Abstract

FlexRL is an R package for record linkage and inference on linked data. It provides a method to link observations from the same entities across two data sources that lack a unique identifier but share partially identifying variables. This method fits a hierarchical latent variable model of the data generation process, rather than classifying pairs of records from the comparison of their values, and accommodates registration errors and variables changing over time. Since linkage may affect subsequent analyses, the package also provides tools to assess the linked data quality as functions of the linkage decision rule. Two false discovery proportion estimators are implemented: a probabilistic approach contingent on the record linkage model and an augmented approach relying on an auxiliary estimation using synthetic data. Several measures of the divergence of the linked sample from the source population are provided. These tools are agnostic to the underlying record linkage model and can be applied to any method. Together, they connect the output of the linkage to the validity of the subsequent analyses. The implementation combines standard R functions and S3 methods with Rcpp computations, resulting in reasonable execution times and a low memory footprint.

Keywords

Record Linkage, Inference, Stochastic EM, False Discovery Proportion, Sampling Selection, Linked Data

1 Introduction

Researchers and statistical agencies increasingly combine data sources that do not share a unique identifier (Cleaton et al. 2024; Laidler et al. 2025; Kramer 2026). Record linkage connects records referring to the same entity across sources based on Partially Identifying Variables (PIVs) (e.g. birth year, sex, postal code or product code, brand and category). Each PIV has limited discriminative power on its own and may be missing, incorrectly recorded or change over time. Record linkage underpins the production of official statistics, enables the reuse of administrative and registry data for research (Collaborative et al. 2020; Pontesilli et al. 2021; Cooney et al. 2023) and improves data quality through linkage and deduplication.

Linked data sets become the study sample for downstream analyses, where linkage errors may propagate (JC and KL 2020; Wang et al. 2022; Vo et al. 2024; Robach et al. 2026). Errors arise when missing links between entities that appear in both sources or when falsely linking records from distinct entities. These errors cannot be reduced simultaneously and may bias the conclusions drawn from linked data (Wang et al. 2022; JC and KL 2020; Robach et al. 2026). A stricter decision rule reduces falsely linked pairs but misses more links. Practitioners therefore need not only record linkage methods, but also tools to quantify linkage errors and appraise their consequences for statistical analysis.

Two main probabilistic approaches to record linkage have developed through times. First, the traditional mixture model (Fellegi and Sunter 1969; Jaro 1989; Larsen 1999; Fortini et al. 2001; Sadinle 2017) treats linkage as a classification problem between pairs of records that are similar and pairs that are not. Second, the hierarchical entity resolution framework (Tancredi and Liseo 2011; Steorts 2015; Steorts et al. 2016; Marchant et al. 2021, 2023) models the data generation process, considering observed values as potentially distorted versions of true latent values. The latter framework uses all the information contained in the records and is therefore more flexible but more computationally demanding. It has been implemented in R for entity resolution (clustering records from a single file into entities) rather than for linkage between two data sources.

We address this gap with the FlexRL R package, which makes two contributions. First, it implements the hierarchical record linkage model proposed by Robach, van der Pas, et al. (2025). The model is fitted with a stochastic expectation maximisation (StEM) algorithm that accounts for registration errors and temporal changes in PIVs. Its architecture and efficient Rcpp backend support scalability to large data sets and a wide range of applications: health monitoring studies, survey waves, census and administrative records or data from different retailers. Second, it provides tools to assess the quality of the linked data, which quantify the linkage error and sample selection. FlexRL implements two estimators of the False Discovery Proportion (FDP): one derived from linkage scores (Belin and Rubin 1995; Resnick and Asher 2019; Enamorado et al. 2019) and one based on synthetic data (Robach, Hof, et al. 2025). FlexRL measures the divergence of the linked sample from the original sources using standardised mean differences, overlap of supports, maximum mean discrepancy and agreement rates. These tools only need the linked pairs and, when available, their linkage scores. They therefore apply to the output of any linkage method and FlexRL provides wrappers for several existing R packages.

The FlexRL package is aimed at practitioners, statistical agencies and researchers who need to link data sources without a unique identifier or who want to measure the error and the sample selection induced by record linkage. It builds on three lines of work: the biases that linkage errors cause in inference (Lahiri and Larsen 2005; Tancredi and Liseo 2015; JC and KL 2020; Wang et al. 2022; Robach et al. 2026); the need for linked data quality control in practice (Harron et al. 2017; Harron K 2020; Lam et al. 2024; Laidler et al. 2025); advances in record linkage methodology (Sadinle 2017; Marchant et al. 2023; Robach, van der Pas, et al. 2025). By combining record linkage and diagnostics in a practical tool, it helps evaluate whether inference can be conducted on linked data.

Throughout the paper, we motivate and illustrate the main components of FlexRL on the 2012 and 2014 waves of the Survey on Household Income and Wealth (SHIW), conducted and made available by the Bank of Italy for research purposes. Section 2 introduces the notation, the modelling paradigms, the existing software and demonstrates the potential of FlexRL. Section 3 presents the record linkage model implemented in FlexRL and its estimation on a subset of the SHIW. On the resulting linked data, Section 4 describes the diagnostic tools FlexRL provides, and illustrates the impact of linkage errors on a socio-economic regression analysis. Section 5 demonstrates the scalability of FlexRL approach to record linkage on the SHIW. Section 6 concludes.

2 The record linkage problem

2.1 Setup and notation

We consider two overlapping samples \(\mathcal A\) and \(\mathcal B\) from the same population with \(N_{\mathcal A}\) and \(N_{\mathcal B} \geq N_{\mathcal A}\) records. Both sources record the same \(K\) categorical PIVs, indexed by \(k = 1, \dots, K\) with \(N_k\) possible values each. Index \(i\) refers to a record in \(\mathcal A\) and index \(j\) to a record in \(\mathcal B.\) For PIV \(k\), we denote by \(G^{\mathcal A}_{i,k}\) the registered (observed) value and by \(H^{\mathcal A}_{i,k}\) the true (latent) value in record \(i \in \mathcal A.\) We define \(G^{\mathcal B}_{j,k}\) and \(H^{\mathcal B}_{j,k}\) analogously. Registered values differ from true values because of registration errors: missing values or mistakes (e.g. typographical errors). We write \(\textbf{G}\) and \(\textbf{H}\) for the collections of all registered and all true values. True values may also differ between the two data collections. This is the case for dynamic PIVs like postal code. Whereas stable PIVs like birth year cannot change. For an entity recorded in both sources, the true values of stable PIVs are therefore identical in \(\mathcal A\) and \(\mathcal B\) and any disagreement between their registered values is due to registration errors. We elaborate on dynamic PIVs later.

The object of interest is the linkage matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Delta} \in \{0,1\}^{N_{\mathcal A} \times N_{\mathcal B}}\), with \(\Delta_{i,j} = 1\) if records \(i \in \mathcal A\) and \(j \in \mathcal B\) refer to the same entity. We assume that there is no duplicate within a data source. Thus, each record forms a link with at most one record in the other data source: \(\sum_j \Delta_{i,j} \leq 1\) for all \(i\) and \(\sum_i \Delta_{i,j} \leq 1\) for all \(j.\) This is the one-to-one assignment constraint, also referred to as bipartite matching (Sadinle 2017). We denote by \(\gamma \in (0,1)\) the probability that a record from \(\mathcal A\) forms a link with a record from \(\mathcal B\), that is, the overlap between the sources as a fraction of the smaller data set.

Since record linkage is imperfect, the linked data contain two types of error: false positives (pairs declared linked while referring to distinct entities) and false negatives (true links that are missed). The non-links in the Cartesian product \(\mathcal A \times \mathcal B\) are far more numerous than the links and are rarely of interest. The useful summaries are therefore the False Discovery Proportion (FDP) among the linked data (complement of precision or positive predictive value) and the sensitivity (also called recall, complement of miss rate). The two errors affect inference differently: falsely linked pairs contaminate the inference and missed links induce sample selection (JC and KL 2020; Robach et al. 2026).

2.2 Modelling paradigms

Linkage methods can be grouped into four paradigms (SB et al. 2014; Binette and Steorts 2022). Deterministic (rule based) linkage declares a link when records agree on a set of variables, exactly or up to a tolerance. Supervised methods train a classifier on pairs whose linkage status is known (Mudgal et al. 2018); such labelled pairs are seldom available. The two remaining paradigms are unsupervised and probabilistic, and differ in what they model:

2.2.1 Paradigm 1: classification of comparison vectors

The traditional approach (Fellegi and Sunter 1969; Dunn 1946; Newcombe et al. 1959; Tepping 1968) reduces the data to comparison vectors. For each pair \((i,j) \in \mathcal A \times \mathcal B\), a vector \(\boldsymbol{c}_{i,j} = (c_{i,j,1}, \dots, c_{i,j,K})\) records whether elements \(G^{\mathcal A}_{i,k}\) and \(G^{\mathcal B}_{j,k}\) for \(k = 1, \dots, K\) agree or how similar they are. The pairs of records are split into the set of matched \(M\) and the set of unmatched \(U.\) The comparison vectors follow a two components mixture: \[ \begin{aligned} P(\boldsymbol{c}_{i,j}) &= \gamma \; m(\boldsymbol{c}_{i,j}) + (1-\gamma) \; u(\boldsymbol{c}_{i,j}), \\ m(\boldsymbol{c}) &= P(\boldsymbol{c} \mid M), \\ u(\boldsymbol{c}) &= P(\boldsymbol{c} \mid U), \end{aligned} \tag{1}\] where \(\gamma\) in this paradigm is the proportion of links among all pairs. Under the assumption that the \(K\) comparisons are independent given the linkage status, \(m\) and \(u\) factorise over \(k \in \{1, \dots, K\}.\) The parameters are then estimated with an EM algorithm (Jaro 1989; Larsen 1999). Pairs are classified by thresholding the ratios \(m(\boldsymbol{c}_{i,j}) / u(\boldsymbol{c}_{i,j})\) or the posterior linkage probabilities. Bayesian versions place a prior on the linkage matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Delta}\) to enforce the one-to-one assignment constraint (Fortini et al. 2001; Sadinle 2017) or a prior on a partition of the records when there are more than two sources or duplicates (Aleshin-Guendel and Sadinle 2023). The computations are simple and fast, but memory intensive because they operate on \(N_{\mathcal A} \times N_{\mathcal B}\) comparison vectors. Scaling to large data sets may therefore require blocking, which compares only pairs that agree on some variables (R. C. Steorts et al. 2014). This paradigm treats pairs as independent although they share records, and the reduction to comparison vectors discards part of the information contained in the records.

2.2.2 Paradigm 2: hierarchical modelling

The second family models how the data were generated (Tancredi and Liseo 2011; Steorts 2015; Steorts et al. 2016; Marchant et al. 2021, 2023; Robach, van der Pas, et al. 2025). Registered values \(\textbf{G}\) are seen as distorted versions of latent true values \(\textbf{H}\), which define the linkage structure \(\boldsymbol{\Delta}.\) The joint model reads \[ P(\textbf{G}, \textbf{H}, \boldsymbol{\Delta}) = P(\textbf{G} \mid \textbf{H}) P(\textbf{H} \mid \boldsymbol{\Delta}) P(\boldsymbol{\Delta}), \tag{2}\] with a model for registration errors \(P(\textbf{G} \mid \textbf{H})\), a model for underlying true values \(P(\textbf{H} \mid \boldsymbol{\Delta})\) and a model for the linkage structure \(P(\boldsymbol{\Delta}).\) Since records are never reduced to pairwise comparisons, the model can separate missing values, mistakes and changes over time, and it does not need blocking to fit in memory. However, it is more computationally demanding.

2.3 Existing software

In Python, recordlinkage (Bruin 2019), Splink (Linacre et al. 2022) and NameMatch (Jelveh et al. 2022) implement Paradigm 1. We list the main R packages for the task considered here in Table 1. Paradigm 2 is only available in R through methods for entity resolution (R. Steorts et al. 2014; Marchant et al. 2021, 2023). These methods cluster records from a single file, possibly containing duplicates, into entities.

To our knowledge, no R package implements Paradigm 2 for the linkage of two sources under the one-to-one assignment constraint or models the dynamics of PIVs over time. While the postlink package (Bukke et al. 2026) develops statistical tools for post-linkage data analysis, no record linkage package provides tools to assess the quality of the linked data and their suitability for inference.

The FlexRL package is available from the Comprehensive R Archive Network (CRAN) or from the package GitHub repository alongside documentation and vignettes describing its functionality. FlexRL is also available as a module in JASP 1.0. The implementation of FlexRL relies on Rcpp for the backend samplers, Matrix for the sparse likelihood and linkage matrix, and other standard R packages. The diagnostic tools apply to any record linkage output in the required format, with built-in wrappers for BRL, diyar, fastLink, fedmatch, multilink and reclin2. 3 The hierarchical model estimation

3.1 Data preparation and PIVs configuration

The record linkage model requires each PIV \(k\) to be categorical, encoded by integers \(1, \dots, N_k\) with missing values coded as \(0.\) The PIVs must cover a common support across the two sources. The smaller source should be labelled as \(\mathcal A.\)

As a first step in the workflow represented in Figure 1, the prepare_data() function which we will describe validates the PIVs configuration, labels the data sources and encodes the PIVs. When a unique identifier is available, like in simulation studies or in benchmark record linkage data such as the SHIW, it also returns the set of true links. This set may be used to evaluate linkage performance.

The function naive_linkage() links records that agree exactly on all PIVs (a missing value matching any value when na_match = TRUE), without enforcing the one-to-one constraint. When the true links are known, the performance of this deterministic linkage indicates the discriminative power of the PIVs and the difficulty of the task. The FDP of the naive linkage reflects how many records share the same combination of PIV values.

The package distinguishes three kinds of PIVs, which determine how the components of the complete data likelihood of Equation 3 are used:

  • Stable: the true values do not change (e.g. birth year). The PIV is used to form candidate pairs (we elaborate on candidate pairs in Section 3.2) and its probability of mistake may be bounded (for example by \(0.10\)) for algorithm stability (Robach, van der Pas, et al. 2025).
  • Dynamic: the true values may change (e.g. postal code between two survey waves):
    • Flexible: changes cannot be modelled, typically because registration dates are not available. The PIV is still used to form candidate pairs, but its probability of mistake should not be bounded, so that the registration model absorbs both mistakes and changes.
    • Structured: changes are modelled by the survival function of Equation 4. The PIV is not used to form candidate pairs. Registration dates are needed in both sources and covariates for the hazard may be supplied. Mistakes cannot be distinguished from temporal changes, so the probability of mistake should be fixed (for example at \(0.02\)), unless enough linked pairs have time gaps near zero, where changes are not plausible and mistakes can be estimated without confusion. Estimating the changes also requires variation in the time gaps among linked pairs.

To illustrate the usage of the FlexRL package throughout the paper, we consider the Emilia-Romagna regional subset of the SHIW data. The record linkage task can be validated in this example where the linkage structure can be reconstructed. The 2012 and 2014 waves respectively cover 1502 and 1538 individuals, with 912 individuals observed in both waves. In Section 4, we analyse a regression of the expected age of retirement in 2014 on the expected age of retirement in 2012.

To link the two survey waves, we use the following variables: year and region of birth, sex, marital status, working status and branch of activity. The strong association between working status and branch of activity violates the PIVs independence assumption of the method and may degrade the record linkage performance (Robach, van der Pas, et al. 2025). We thus combine these variables into a single variable ‘occupation’. Although marital status and occupation are ‘dynamic’ PIVs, we do not have enough information to model their temporal changes and set them as ‘flexible’ in the configuration:

3.2 StEM estimation

The FlexRL hierarchical model for record linkage adapts Equation 2 to two sources under the one-to-one assignment constraint. For non-linked records, the true values are independent draws from the population distribution. For a linked pair \((i,j)\), \(\boldsymbol H^{\mathcal B}_{j,k}\) is a copy of \(\boldsymbol H^{\mathcal A}_{i,k}\) except when \(k\) is a dynamic PIV, whose values may have changed between the two registrations. The registered values are versions of the true values distorted by registration errors.

With parameters \(\boldsymbol{\theta} = (\gamma, \boldsymbol{\eta}, \boldsymbol{\alpha}, \boldsymbol{\phi})\), the complete data likelihood factorises as \[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \big( \textbf{G}^{\mathcal{A}}, \textbf{G}^{\mathcal{B}}, \textbf{H}^{\mathcal{A}}, \textbf{H}^{\mathcal{B}}, \boldsymbol{\Delta} \big) = & \underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\mathcal A}} \big( \textbf{G}^{\mathcal{A}} \mid \textbf{H}^{\mathcal{A}} \big) \mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\mathcal B}} \big( \textbf{G}^{\mathcal{B}} \mid \textbf{H}^{\mathcal{B}} \big)}_{\text{registration errors}} \\ & \underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}, \boldsymbol{\eta}} \big( \textbf{H}^{\mathcal{B}} \mid \textbf{H}^{\mathcal{A}}, \boldsymbol{\Delta} \big)}_{\text{changes over time}} \underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}} \big( \textbf{H}^{\mathcal{A}} \big)}_{\text{true values}} \\ & \underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\gamma} \big( \boldsymbol{\Delta} \big)}_{\text{linkage}} . \end{aligned} \tag{3}\] Each component is developed in the original methodological paper (Robach, van der Pas, et al. 2025).

Notably, the changes over time are modelled with a survival function, representing the probability that true values have not changed between data collections. By default, the package uses an exponential model with proportional hazards. For a dynamic structured PIV \(k\) whose changes are modelled and a linked pair \((i,j)\) with time gap \(t_{i,j} = |t^{\mathcal B}_j - t^{\mathcal A}_i|\): \[ P\big(H^{\mathcal B}_{j,k} = H^{\mathcal A}_{i,k} \mid t_{i,j}, \boldsymbol{x}_{i,j}, \Delta_{i,j} = 1\big) = S_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i,j}, t_{i,j}) = \exp\big\{ - \exp(\boldsymbol{x}_{i,j}^{T} \boldsymbol{\alpha}_k) t_{i,j} \big\}, \tag{4}\] where \(\boldsymbol{x}_{i,j}\) contains an intercept and, optionally, covariates from either data source that explain the hazard of change (for example age or income for a relocation) and \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_k\) contains the log hazard coefficients. The user can choose another survival model, such as a Weibull, a Gompertz, a piecewise constant hazard or any custom model.

The likelihood of the observed data is given by Equation 3, summed over all values of the latent \(\textbf{H}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Delta}.\) FlexRL StEM() function maximises the likelihood with a stochastic EM algorithm (Celeux and Diebolt 1986). The E-step draws the latent variables from their distribution given the current parameters with a Gibbs sampler and the M-step maximises the resulting complete data likelihood. Algorithm 1 summarises the procedure.

Algorithm 1  

Input: encoded sources G_A, G_B; PIV configuration;
       N_StEM iterations (burn-in N0_StEM); N_Gibbs iterations (burn-in N0_Gibbs)
Initialise θ = (γ, η, α, φ)
for StEM iter = 1, ..., N_StEM do
    Δ ← 0
    # stochastic E-step
    for Gibbs iter = 1, ..., N_Gibbs do          
        draw H_A, H_B | G_A, G_B, Δ, θ, x, t
        form candidate pairs (i,j) such that H_A[i,k] = H_B[j,k] for all stable or flexible k
        for each non-linked candidate pair: propose to link
        for each linked pair: propose to break the link
        accept each proposal with the likelihood of the model
        if Gibbs iter > N0_Gibbs then
            store: count of linked data, true values crosstab, agreements G = H, x, t
        end if
    end for
    # M-step
    update and store θ = (γ, η, α, φ)
end for
θ_hat ← average of θ over StEM iter > N0_StEM
run further Gibbs iterations at θ_hat to build D_hat:
D_hat[i,j] ← proportion of iterations in which (i,j) is linked

Stochastic EM for record linkage.

3.2.1 Initialisation

The probability \(\gamma\) is drawn uniformly in \((0.2, 0.8)\), the distributions of each PIV \(k\), \(\boldsymbol\eta_k\), are uniform over the \(N_k\) values, the probabilities of mistakes are drawn in \((0.03, 0.20)\) and the probabilities of missing values are set to the observed proportions. For structured PIVs, the log baseline hazard is drawn between \(0.01\) and \(1\) per time unit and the covariate coefficients near \(0.\) The user can supply other starting values.

3.2.2 Stochastic E-step

Given \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\), the Gibbs sampler alternates two blocks. First, the true values are drawn. For a non-linked record \(H^{\mathcal A}_{i,k}\) or \(H^{\mathcal B}_{j,k}\) is drawn from \(\boldsymbol\eta_k\) weighted by the registration model. For a linked pair \((H^{\mathcal A}_{i,k}, H^{\mathcal B}_{j,k})\) is drawn jointly among the \(N_k \times N_k\) possible values, from \(\boldsymbol\eta_k\) weighted by the registration model and the survival model. Second, the linkage is updated. Candidate pairs are the pairs whose sampled true values agree on all PIVs that are not structured. For these, we compute the log likelihood contribution of the pair when linked, \(\log \mathcal L^{L}_{i,j}\), and of the two records when not linked, \(\log \mathcal L^{\mathcal A}_i + \log \mathcal L^{\mathcal B}_j.\) A sequential sweep over the candidate pairs then proposes to create or break links. A proposal is accepted with probability \(\mathcal L_{\text{new}} / (\mathcal L_{\text{old}} + \mathcal L_{\text{new}})\), where the two log likelihoods differ by \(\log \mathcal L^{L}_{i,j} - \log \mathcal L^{\mathcal A}_i - \log \mathcal L^{\mathcal B}_j\) and by the contributions from changes in the linkage matrix.

Restricting the sweep to candidate pairs keeps the algorithm light in memory: likelihood contributions are computed only for these pairs and for the remaining non-linked records in each data source, which together represent the estimated unique entities in the population.

3.2.3 M-step

The summary statistics averaged over the Gibbs draws after burn-in give the parameters updates. Thanks to the factorisation of Equation 3, \(\gamma\), \(\boldsymbol{\eta}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\phi}\) have separate closed form updates, and \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) is obtained by numerical optimisation. Optional bounds on the probabilities of a mistake are applied at this stage. A warning is displayed when a bound is reached in many iterations, suggesting that the bound is too tight or that the PIV is dynamic.

3.3 Linkage scores and decision rule

Based on the StEM chains of estimates, we build an estimate of the set of parameters, \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\), by averaging the post burn-in elements in the sequence. These are used to sample further linkage matrices that are averaged to return a probabilistic estimate of \(\boldsymbol{\Delta}\) as a final output, namely \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{D}}\), whose entries \(\widehat{D}_{i,j}\) are the posterior probabilities for the pair \((i,j)\) to be linked, which we call the linkage scores. A decision rule on each entry \(\xi \in [0.5,1)\) then defines the final set of linked data \(\mathcal{D} = \{(i,j) ; \widehat{D}_{i,j} > \xi\}.\) Since the scores of a record sum to at most one, a threshold of at least \(0.5\) ensures the one-to-one assignment (Tancredi and Liseo 2011; Sadinle 2017). The choice of \(\xi\) is the subject of the upcoming Section 4.

Note that, probabilistic record linkage methods estimate \(\boldsymbol{\Delta}\) in comparable ways, by classifying pairs according to their linkage scores. Although these scores may be on different scales (e.g. likelihood ratios, log odds, posterior probabilities), they can be mapped to a probability scale. Hereafter, we take linkage scores to be posterior linkage probabilities in \([0,1]\) and treat all the methods listed in Table 1 in the same way.

3.4 Record linkage with StEM()

StEM() runs Algorithm 1. Its main arguments are:

  • data: a list, typically the output of prepare_data(), with the encoded sources encodedA and encodedB, the number of distinct values of each PIV n_values (excluding missing values), a logical indicating whether both sources share the registration error parameters same_mistakes, and the PIVs configuration PIVs_config.
  • StEM_iter, StEM_burnin, gibbs_iter, gibbs_burnin: the numbers of iterations of the outer StEM algorithm and of the inner Gibbs sampler, and their burn-in periods. gibbs_burnin = 0 sets the burn-in automatically: the sampler runs until the number of links changes by fewer than 10 for 10 consecutive iterations, with a maximum of 500 iterations.
  • model_dynamics: the survival model for structured PIVs, by default the exponential model of Equation 4. It is only used if a PIV is declared structured in PIVs_config.
  • n_post_sample: the number of posterior draws used to compute the linkage scores.
  • Starting values for \(\gamma\) and for the agreement probabilities \(1-\boldsymbol\phi\) can be supplied.
  • For long runs, new_directory and save_info_iter = TRUE save the environment after each StEM iteration, and music_on = TRUE plays a short tune when the algorithm finishes running.

The function returns Delta, a data frame with columns i (indices in \(\mathcal A\)), j (indices in \(\mathcal B\)) and x (scores \(\widehat{D}_{i,j}\)) for every pair with a positive score, and the four chains: gamma (a vector of length StEM_iter), eta (one StEM_iter\(\times N_k\) matrix per PIV), alpha (one matrix per structured PIV, with StEM_iter rows and one column per parameter of the survival model) and phi (one StEM_iter\(\times 4\) matrix per PIV: agreement in \(\mathcal A\), in \(\mathcal B\), missing in \(\mathcal A\), in \(\mathcal B\)).

We run the FlexRL StEM algorithm for record linkage as follows:

fit <- StEM(data = prep_data)

3.5 Computational aspects

The two latent variable samplers are implemented with Rcpp. To draw the true values, sampleH() goes through the \(N_k^2\) joint values once per PIV \(k\) for linked pairs and through the \(N_k\) values once per PIV \(k\) for non-linked records. sampleD() performs the sequential sweep over candidate pairs and stores the linkage as a hash map, so that the cost of a sweep is nearly linear in the number of candidate pairs and the memory is linear in the number of links built rather than in \(N_{\mathcal A} \times N_{\mathcal B}.\)

Candidate pairs are found by hashing the pattern of sampled true values on the PIVs that are not structured, an operation linear in \(N_{\mathcal A} + N_{\mathcal B}\), and their likelihood contributions are stored in a sparse matrix. Memory therefore grows with the number of records and of candidate pairs, not with \(N_{\mathcal A} \times N_{\mathcal B}.\) Running time grows with the number of records; the number of candidate pairs, larger when PIVs have few levels; the number of PIVs and their numbers of levels; the number of structured PIVs, whose survival model is estimated at each iteration; the number of iterations. All random draws use the random number generator of R, so that set.seed() makes a run reproducible.

4 Inference on linked data

4.1 Impact of linkage errors on linear regression

Suppose we are interested in estimating the association between \(X\) recorded in \(\mathcal A\) and \(Y\) recorded in \(\mathcal B\), for instance through the slope \(\beta\) of the linear regression \(Y = X\beta + \varepsilon\), with \(\mathbb E[\varepsilon]=0\) and \(\mathbb E[X \varepsilon]=0\), so that \[\widehat \beta = \operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)/\operatorname{Var}(X). \tag{5}\] The target is \(\beta\) in the population of entities present in both sources. The analyst only observes the linked data \(\mathcal{D} = \{(i,j) ; \widehat{D}_{i,j} > \xi\}\) with \(X_i\) from \(\mathcal A\) and \(Y_j\) from \(\mathcal B.\) \(Y_j\) equals \(Y_i\) for truly linked pairs but belongs to another entity for falsely linked records. Among the linked data, a proportion \(1-\text{FDP}\) (that may in reality depend on \(X\)) carries the correct \(Y\) and a proportion \(\text{FDP}\) carries a spurious one. Below we denote by \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\) the estimator of \(\boldsymbol{\Delta}\), with \(\widehat{\Delta}_{i,j} = 1\) for pairs \((i,j)\) in \(\mathcal{D}.\) Truly linked pairs of records (true positives, TP) are thus denoted \(\{ \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Delta}} = 1, \boldsymbol{\Delta} = 1 \}\) and falsely linked pairs of records (false positives, FP) \(\{ \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Delta}} = 1, \boldsymbol{\Delta} = 0 \}.\) Heuristically, \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E[ Y \mid X, \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Delta}} = 1 ] & = (1-\text{FDP}) \cdot \mathbb E[ Y \mid X, \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Delta}} = 1, \boldsymbol{\Delta} = 1 ] + \text{FDP} \cdot \mathbb E[ Y \mid X, \widehat{\boldsymbol{\Delta}} = 1, \boldsymbol{\Delta} = 0] \\ & = (1-\text{FDP}) \cdot \mathbb E_{TP}[ Y \mid X ] + \text{FDP} \cdot \mathbb E_{FP}[ Y \mid X ], \end{aligned} \] where \(\mathbb E_{TP}\) and \(\mathbb E_{FP}\) denote expectations among truly and falsely linked pairs.

Let us recast \(\widehat\beta\) accordingly. If \(X\) has the same mean and variance among all data in \(\mathcal{A}\), then \(\widehat\beta\) is a mixture of \(\widehat\beta_{TP}\) and \(\widehat\beta_{FP}\), defined like in Equation 5 for the subsets of TP and FP, \[ \widehat\beta = (1-\text{FDP}) \cdot \widehat\beta_{TP} + \text{FDP} \cdot \widehat\beta_{FP}. \tag{6}\] Linkage errors are often assumed to occur at random, in which case \(\mathbb E_{FP}[ Y \mid X ] = \mathbb E_{FP}[ Y ]\) and \(\widehat\beta_{FP} = 0.\) However, the PIVs, often demographic, are likely to be associated with variables of interest in the analysis, so it is more realistic to consider an association between \(X\) and \(Y\) among falsely linked pairs. Hence, the error of the analysis splits into two parts: \[ \widehat\beta - \beta = \underbrace{(\widehat\beta_{TP} - \beta)}_{\text{sampling bias}} \:\:\: + \underbrace{(\widehat\beta - \widehat\beta_{TP})}_{\text{contamination bias}}. \tag{7}\] Missed links may cause sample selection and falsely linked data may cause measurement error in the linked variables (Wang et al. 2022; JC and KL 2020; Robach et al. 2026).

4.1.1 Linkage decision rule trade-off

Raising the threshold \(\xi\) on the linkage scores reduces the number of falsely linked pairs, which lowers the FDP and hence the contamination bias. Falsely linked data would distort the signal in proportion to the FDP. Indeed, from Equation 6 we derive: \[ \underbrace{\widehat\beta - \widehat\beta_{TP}}_{\text{contamination bias}} = \text{FDP} (\widehat\beta_{FP} - \widehat\beta_{TP}). \tag{8}\]

However, raising the threshold \(\xi\) also selects fewer and more atypical records, which may increase the sampling bias due to the implicit record linkage selection. Indeed, the chance of being linked may be related to \(X\) and as a consequence: \[ \underbrace{\widehat\beta_{TP} - \beta}_{\text{sampling bias}} = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}_{TP}(X,\varepsilon)}{\operatorname{Var}_{TP}(X)}. \tag{9}\]

These aspects motivate the complementary tools we develop in FlexRL to quantify these two biases. Estimating the FDP (see Section 4.2) and evaluating the divergence of the linked sample from the data sources (see Section 4.3) over a range of values of \(\xi\) may help users appraise this trade-off.

4.2 False discovery proportion

For the decision rule \(\xi \in [0.5, 1)\), the FDP is \(\text{FDP}(\xi) = \text{FP}(\xi) / \{\text{TP}(\xi) + \text{FP}(\xi)\}\) where \(\text{TP}(\xi)\) and \(\text{FP}(\xi)\) are the numbers of truly and falsely linked pairs. In real data applications this quantity is unknown. Linkage errors can be estimated from the scores of the linkage model (Belin and Rubin 1995; Resnick and Asher 2019; Enamorado et al. 2019), from synthetic data (Lam et al. 2024; Robach, Hof, et al. 2025) or from a separate model under conditions on the distribution of the number of records per entity (Blakely and Salmond 2002; Dasylva and Goussanou 2020, 2022). FlexRL implements the first two estimation procedures. We focus on the FDP because it drives the contamination bias of Equation 8; the sampling bias is addressed by the diagnostics of Section 4.3. The estimators are provided as functions of \(\xi\), together with the linked sample size available for inference, so that the user can choose a threshold controlling the estimated FDP (and hopefully, the true underlying FDP).

4.2.1 Score based estimator

If the linkage model is well calibrated, \(\widehat{D}_{i,j}\) should represent the probability that the pair \((i,j)\) is a true link, and the expected number of truly linked pairs among linked data is \(\sum_{(i,j)} \widehat{D}_{i,j} \mathbb 1 \{\widehat{D}_{i,j} > \xi\}.\) Therefore \[ \widehat{\text{FDP}}(\xi) = 1 - \frac{\sum_{(i,j)} \widehat{D}_{i,j} \mathbb 1 \{\widehat{D}_{i,j} > \xi\}}{\sum_{(i,j)} \mathbb 1 \{\widehat{D}_{i,j} > \xi\}} . \tag{10}\] This estimator comes at no extra cost for any method that returns linkage scores, but it is only as good as the linkage model. The calibration of the scores is affected by missing values and mistakes, the size of the overlap between the sources, the number of entities in the population, the discriminative power of the PIVs, their changes over time, and their marginal and joint distributions.

4.2.2 Synthetic data estimator

An estimator that does not rely on linkage scores can be computed with synthetic data (Robach, Hof, et al. 2025). Source \(\mathcal B\) is augmented with \(N_{\text{synth}}\) synthetic records, drawn from a generative model fitted on the PIVs of \(\mathcal B\)—for example arf (Watson et al. 2023), synthpop (Nowok et al. 2016) or mice (Buuren and Groothuis-Oudshoorn 2011). Synthetic records by definition do not form a link in \(\mathcal A\), so synthetic links can represent false positives when links and non-links have similar distributions (Robach, Hof, et al. 2025). Then \(\text{FP}(\xi)\) is estimated by scaling the number of synthetic links by \(N_{\mathcal B} / N_{\text{synth}}\) and, like for the score based estimator, \(\text{TP}(\xi) + \text{FP}(\xi)\) is estimated by the number of (real) linked pairs \(N_{\text{real}}(\xi) = \sum_{(i,j)} \mathbb 1 \{\widehat{D}_{i,j} > \xi\}\): \[ \widehat{\text{FDP}}_{\text{synth}}(\xi) = \frac{\text{FP}_{\text{synth}}(\xi) \cdot N_{\mathcal B} / N_{\text{synth}}}{N_{\text{real}}(\xi)}. \tag{11}\] The procedure is repeated over several synthetic draws and the results are averaged. The estimator does not depend on the linkage model, but it depends on the quality of the data synthesis.

4.3 Representativeness of the linked sample

As explained in Section 4.1, the linked sample may differ from the data sources in ways the analyst cannot see (JC and KL 2020; Wang et al. 2022; Robach et al. 2026). In particular, we appraise the sampling bias of Equation 9 by comparing the distributions of variables of interest between the linked data (‘selected’ through record linkage) and the original data sources. For any decision rule \(\xi \in [0.5, 1)\), FlexRL compares each source with its linked subset using the following measures, computed on sets of variables \(\mathcal V_{\mathcal A}\) and \(\mathcal V_{\mathcal B}\) available in sources \(\mathcal A\) and \(\mathcal B\) respectively:

  • Agreement rates: the proportion of linked pairs whose records agree on \(v\), for each variable \(v \in \mathcal V_{\mathcal A} \cap \mathcal V_{\mathcal B}\) (such as the PIVs).
  • Standardised mean differences: \((\text{mean}(v_{\text{linked}}) - \text{mean}(v_{\text{source}})) / \text{sd}(v_{\text{source}})\), for a continuous variable \(v\) in a given set; the same quantity is computed for the indicator of each level when \(v\) is categorical.
  • Overlap of supports: the intersection over union of the sets of values taken in the linked sample and in the source, for a variable \(v\) in a given set.
  • Maximum mean discrepancy: a multivariate measure of the difference between the linked sample and the source, computed jointly over a given set of variables, with a Gaussian kernel and the median heuristic for the bandwidth (Gretton et al. 2012).

These quantities are computed over a grid of values of \(\xi.\) They are built on the declared pairs and, when available, their linkage scores; they therefore apply to any linkage method.

6 Discussion

This article presented FlexRL: an R package for probabilistic record linkage of two overlapping data sources using a hierarchical model that accounts for registration errors and accommodates temporal changes in Partially Identifying Variables (PIVs). FlexRL provides tools for assessing the quality of the resulting linked data. These diagnostics are independent of the linkage method and thus apply broadly. These tools are essential for using record linkage in practice, where potential linkage errors and induced sample selection cannot be observed directly.

To draw the attention to the implications of linkage quality for statistical analysis, FlexRL provides two complementary tools as functions of the linkage threshold. First, the estimated False Discovery Proportion (FDP), which quantifies contamination among the linked pairs. Second, the divergence of the linked sample from the source populations, which captures potential sampling bias. Examining these quantities jointly makes the trade-off between contamination and sampling bias explicit and provides users with information to select a linkage decision rule in light of its potential consequences for subsequent inference.

Several directions follow naturally from this work. On the computational side, the memory footprint is nearly linear in the number of links but the running time could be further improved, as it scales with the number of records, candidate pairs, PIVs, their levels and configurations. On the modelling side, the linkage model currently assumes independence of PIVs given the true values, no duplicates within a source, categorical PIVs; extensions would broaden the range of applications. Finally, the diagnostic estimators and the proposed quality measures, whose validity rests on the assumptions underlying their estimation, would benefit from further theoretical and empirical investigation.

Notwithstanding these considerations, FlexRL provides a framework that connects record linkage to linked data quality assessment. It provides a scalable and reliable solution to record linkage in terms of memory usage and performance on a variety of scenarios. It enables record linkage to reach practice, helping researchers and practitioners not only to link data, but also quantify and communicate the extent to which linkage decisions may affect downstream inference.
